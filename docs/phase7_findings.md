# Phase 7 — Python EDA: Key Findings

Full computed statistics: [`reports/phase7_eda_stats.md`](../reports/phase7_eda_stats.md).
Figures: [`reports/figures/phase7/`](../reports/figures/phase7/). Generated by
[`python/eda.py`](../python/eda.py). As in prior phases, findings are labeled
**Fact** or **Observation**; every number below is computed directly from
`data/processed/`, not estimated.

## Order & Customer Spend Distributions

* **Fact:** Order revenue is strongly **right-skewed** (skewness = 3.51):
  mean order value ($2,117.89) is nearly **double** the median ($1,146.00).
  Most orders are modest; a smaller number of large orders pull the average
  up. See `01_order_revenue_distribution.png`.
* **Fact:** Using a standard IQR outlier rule (Q3 + 1.5×IQR = $6,125.04),
  **7.37% of orders** (1,939) are statistical outliers, but they account for
  **34.58% of total revenue** ($19.28M). These are legitimate large orders
  (e.g. desktops, TVs, appliances bought in quantity), not data errors —
  confirmed against actual line items, not assumed.
* **Fact:** Customer total-spend distribution is similarly right-skewed
  (skewness = 2.31): median customer spend is $2,995 vs. mean $4,690.46.
  See `03_customer_spend_distribution.png` — the log-scale panel shows the
  distribution is closer to log-normal than the linear view suggests.
* **Observation:** Both the order-level and customer-level skew, together
  with the moderate revenue concentration already found in Phase 6 (top
  10% of customers = 35.98% of revenue), paint a consistent picture: this
  is a business with a long tail of modest transactions and a meaningful
  minority of large ones — not two separate customer populations, but one
  continuous, skewed spending distribution.

## Line Items per Order

* **Fact:** Orders average **2.39 line items** (median 2, max 7); the most
  common order size is a single item (mode = 1). See
  `02_line_items_per_order.png`. This confirms Phase 6's repeat-purchase
  findings are about *separate orders over time*, not large multi-item
  basket sizes.

## Product-Level: Quantity vs. Revenue

* **Fact:** Across 2,492 products with at least one sale, the correlation
  between total units sold and total revenue is **0.515** — positive but
  moderate, not near 1.0. See `04_quantity_vs_revenue_by_product.png`.
* **Observation:** A correlation well below 1.0 confirms what Phase 5
  already showed directly (top-by-quantity and top-by-revenue product
  lists only partially overlap): unit price varies enough across the
  catalog that selling a lot of units and earning a lot of revenue are
  related but distinct signals — both should be tracked, not just one.

## Seasonality (Confirmed Visually and Numerically)

* **Fact:** Averaging across the 5 full years (2016–2020), **April is by
  far the lowest-revenue month** (avg $121,466.81) and **December the
  highest** (avg $1,499,321.77) — a >12x gap. February is also
  unexpectedly strong (avg $1,463,241.87), close behind December. See
  `06_seasonality_by_month.png`.
* **Fact:** Revenue by day of week is highly uneven: **Saturday**
  generates the most revenue ($13.20M total) and **Sunday** dramatically
  the least ($0.91M) — roughly a 14x gap. This pattern was **verified
  using order counts** (not just revenue) and holds **separately for both
  online and in-store channels** (e.g. Saturday: 4,931 in-store + 1,333
  online orders vs. Sunday: 363 + 90), so it is not an artifact of
  physical-store closures alone. See `07_revenue_by_weekday.png`.
* **Observation:** The April dip is consistent enough across 5 separate
  years to be a real recurring seasonal pattern rather than noise, though
  this dataset offers no explanatory field (promotions calendar, fiscal
  year structure) to say *why* April specifically underperforms.

## Category Performance (Distribution View)

* **Fact:** `08_revenue_by_category_boxplot.png` shows line-item revenue
  spread by category. Categories like Computers and TV and Video have
  visibly wider interquartile ranges (more variable transaction sizes)
  than smaller categories like Games and Toys, consistent with their
  wider price ranges in the product catalog.

## Regional (Customer-Side) Revenue

* **Fact:** `09_revenue_by_country.png` visually confirms the Phase 5/6
  finding: United States dominates, followed by a second tier (UK,
  Germany, Canada) roughly a third to a quarter of the US total each.

## Customer Age

* **Fact:** Among purchasing customers, mean age (as of the latest order
  date in the dataset) is **52.3 years**, median 52.4, ranging from 19.1
  to 86.0. See `10_customer_age_distribution.png`. Age was not examined
  in Phase 5/6 SQL analytics — this is new ground covered in Python EDA.
* **Observation:** The distribution is centered in middle age with no
  strong skew toward younger or older customers — useful context if
  age-based segmentation is considered later, though this project's core
  segmentation approach (Phase 8) is behavior-based (RFM), not
  demographic.

## Outlier Analysis (Line-Item Level)

* **Fact:** At the individual line-item level, 9.11% of rows (5,729)
  exceed the IQR outlier fence ($2,250.00). The single highest line-item
  revenue is $31,999.90 (10 units of a Litware Refrigerator). Manually
  checking this and other top outliers confirms they are legitimate
  bulk/high-price purchases, not data entry errors — consistent with the
  "no outliers required removal" decision already made in Phase 3.

## Summary: What Python EDA Added Beyond SQL

* Confirmed skew/outlier structure numerically (skewness, IQR fences) —
  SQL aggregates alone don't show distribution shape.
* Found the **day-of-week pattern** (Saturday high / Sunday very low) —
  not examined in Phase 5/6, and non-obvious from monthly SQL rollups.
* Quantified the **quantity-vs-revenue correlation** (0.515) rather than
  just eyeballing two separate top-10 lists.
* Added a **new dimension** (customer age) not covered in the SQL phases.
